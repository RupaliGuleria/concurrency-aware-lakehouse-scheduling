A lakehouse scheduling study

It's not the data flood. It's the crowd.

A year spent testing one widely held assumption about why business dashboards slow down at the worst possible moment — and what actually fixes it.

Rupali Guleria·Independent Researcher

Picture a retail company on its biggest sale day of the year. Orders are pouring in. Somewhere in another tab, an operations lead is watching a live dashboard — revenue by hour, stock levels, which warehouse is falling behind — and it keeps stalling. Everyone in the room reaches the same conclusion without discussing it: it's slow because so much new data is coming in right now.

That assumption is reasonable. It's also, for one real system tested here, wrong.

The assumption everyone makes

Modern analytics systems increasingly use a "lakehouse" architecture — a setup where the computing engine that answers your questions and the storage holding your data are two separate systems, connected over a network, often sharing the same cluster with whatever process is busy writing new data in. Trino is one of the most widely used engines of this kind; it's the query layer behind analytics at companies handling exactly this mix of constant writes and constant questions.

The natural first fix, when that dashboard crawls, is to build a scheduler that watches how much new data is streaming in and reorders queries around it — run the quick ones first when ingestion is heavy, hold the expensive ones for a quieter moment. It's what this project set out to build, too.

Testing it, four separate ways

Before building that scheduler, the assumption underneath it got tested directly: does a flood of incoming data actually slow queries down enough to matter? A real Trino deployment was run with a standard analytical workload — point lookups, aggregations, joins, the kind of questions a business dashboard actually asks — while a separate stream of synthetic transaction data was fed in at varying intensity, from none to heavy and sustained.

Four independent tests, run at different stages of the project using different methods, landed on the same answer.

The data flood wasn't the bottleneck. Not in this setup, not at this scale. A scheduler built around it would have been solving the wrong problem.

What was actually happening

The thing that did move the needle was simpler and easy to miss: how many queries were running at the same moment. Not what they were doing — just how many of them, at once, competing for the same fixed pool of CPU and I/O.

1 query running
19×slower
5,717ms

Same query. Same question asked of the same data. The only thing that changed was how many other people were asking their own questions at the same instant — which is exactly what a sale-day dashboard looks like: everyone refreshing together, not one big delivery of new data arriving at once.

Teaching the scheduler to notice the crowd

With the real driver identified, the scheduler got rebuilt around it. The core idea is a rule called least slack: of everything waiting to run, do the one closest to blowing its deadline first. The original version of this rule always assumed the system was idle — a best-case guess. The rebuilt version checks how busy the system actually is right now and adjusts its time estimate accordingly, the way a delivery app updates your ETA once it sees real traffic instead of assuming empty roads.

That adjustment alone changed real decisions: in simulation, the busy-aware version picked a different running order than the idle-assuming one in 488 out of 1,000 batches. A second version went further, using an exact optimization method (dynamic programming) to compute the mathematically best possible order for a batch, rather than a solid rule of thumb — useful for measuring exactly how much room a smarter algorithm actually has to improve on the simple heuristic.

The bouncer at the door

Past a certain number of simultaneous queries, no amount of clever reordering helps. There simply isn't enough machine to go around, and every scheduler tested — including the mathematically optimal one — eventually hit 0% success at high enough load. Shuffling the line doesn't help when the room is already full.

The more useful response, the paper argues, isn't a smarter line. It's a door.

Before admitting a new query, check whether letting it in would still leave everything already admitted with a fair shot at its deadline. If not, that query waits — it isn't dropped, just run later, after the ones already let in.

This is the paper's admission control mechanism: a feasibility check at the door, not a smarter queue inside the room.

Tested live, not just on paper

A simulation proving this works is one kind of evidence. The stronger kind is watching it hold up against a real, running system under real concurrent load — which is what happened next.

82.5%
of arriving queries deferred, live, once the system judged itself overloaded under heavy real concurrency
0 of 5
live conditions where admitted queries did worse than the unfiltered average

In every live condition tested, the queries that got let in met their deadlines more often than the blended average would have suggested — including the deliberately deferred ones scored honestly, as ordinary misses, once they finally ran last.

Where it fell short

One result is reported without softening it. Under the single heaviest live condition tested, the admitted queries' success rate landed at 33.3%, well under the system's own 80% target — the one case where the mechanism's confidence in itself didn't match reality.

The paper traces why: its feasibility predictions are built from measurements taken in short, discrete rounds, while this live test ran under continuous background load — a denser, steadier kind of busy than the calibration data expected. A direct measurement of prediction error confirms it: at that exact condition, the predictor's error rate was roughly four times higher than everywhere else it was tested. The gap is real, it's measured, and it's reported rather than quietly dropped from the results.

Why this matters past one dashboard

The specific numbers here come from one machine, one workload, three coarse levels of "how busy." They aren't a universal law. What travels further is the method: a popular assumption, tested directly before anything was built around it, and a result that overturned the premise the project started with.

That pattern — the moment a system is suddenly asked for everything, by everyone, at once — isn't specific to lakehouses. It's payday in a banking app, checkout during a flash sale, a results page the morning grades come out. The systems that stay calm through those moments usually aren't the ones that got smarter about ordering the line. They're the ones that learned when to say, politely, not yet.
